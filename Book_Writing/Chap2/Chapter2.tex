\chapter{Literature Review and Research Gaps}
\label{chap:literature_review}

Computational models powered by Machine Learning (ML) have emerged as cornerstone assets for automated clinical screening, diagnostic triaging, and electronic health record analysis~\cite{shortliffe2018clinical, topol2019high}. A substantial body of academic literature demonstrates that supervised algorithms can attain high mathematical precision when mapping structured patient symptoms to disease targets. However, significant methodological, clinical, and statistical gaps persist between published experimental benchmarks and real-world clinical applicability. This chapter surveys the relevant literature across six foundational thematic domains and articulates the specific research gaps addressed in this thesis.

\section{Evolution of Clinical Decision Support: From Expert Systems to Statistical AI}
\label{sec:cdss_evolution}

The ambition to automate medical diagnosis originated during the early decades of artificial intelligence. First-generation Clinical Decision Support Systems (CDSS) were predominantly expert systems relying on handcrafted, deterministic production rules. The landmark MYCIN system, pioneered by Shortliffe~\cite{shortliffe1976computer}, utilized roughly 450 backward-chaining rules and ad-hoc certainty factors to recommend antimicrobial therapy for infectious bacteremia and meningitis. Similarly, the INTERNIST-I project developed by Miller et al.~\cite{miller1982internist} modeled broad internal medicine pathology through heuristic disease-manifestation networks covering over 500 clinical diagnoses. While these pioneering systems demonstrated the conceptual feasibility of algorithmic reasoning, their clinical deployment was severely hindered by knowledge acquisition bottlenecks, inability to handle missing evidence gracefully, and high maintenance overhead.

With the proliferation of digitized medical records and standardized vocabularies, clinical AI transitioned from rule curation toward statistical learning and supervised machine learning~\cite{shickel2017deep}. Contemporary patient-facing symptom checkers have achieved widespread consumer adoption. However, empirical investigations evaluate their diagnostic precision critically: in a landmark audit of 23 commercial symptom checkers, Semigran et al.~\cite{semigran2015evaluation} revealed that algorithmic triage tools correctly identified the primary diagnosis in only 34\% of standardized clinical vignettes (improving to 58\% within the Top-20 candidates). More recently, a comprehensive meta-analysis by Elias et al.~\cite{elias2021diagnostic} confirmed substantial heterogeneity across digital triage systems, emphasizing that patient-reported presentation sparsity remains an unaddressed failure mode in existing diagnostic engines.

\section{Supervised Learning Architectures for Tabular Clinical Data}
\label{sec:supervised_architectures}

Unlike computer vision or natural language processing, where deep convolutional and transformer architectures dominate, tabular symptom-disease classification remains primarily governed by specialized decision-tree ensembles and regularized linear baselines~\cite{pedregosa2011scikit}.

Random Forests, formulated by Breiman~\cite{breiman2001random}, combine bootstrap aggregating (bagging) with random feature subspace sampling, creating robust, variance-reduced ensembles that resist overfitting on binary symptom profiles. Geurts et al.~\cite{geurts2006extremely} extended this concept through Extremely Randomized Trees (Extra Trees), which select cut-points completely at random, further reducing variance at the expense of a modest increase in bias. For structured gradient boosting, Chen and Guestrin's XGBoost~\cite{chen2016xgboost} introduced second-order Taylor approximations to the loss function coupled with column subsampling and algorithmic cache awareness, achieving state-of-the-art performance across competitive tabular benchmarks.

Concurrently, maximum-margin classifiers such as Support Vector Machines (SVM)~\cite{cortes1995support} map sparse symptom vectors into high-dimensional Hilbert spaces using radial basis function (RBF) kernels. In parallel, deep neural architectures for tabular healthcare records~\cite{shickel2017deep} apply non-linear Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs) with dropout regularization to approximate complex symptom co-occurrences. Nonetheless, the comparative resilience of these competing paradigms under clinical degradation regimes (such as severe symptom omission or spurious noise) has not been systematically investigated on shared diagnostic benchmarks.

\section{The Pitfall of Data Leakage and the Reproducibility Crisis}
\label{sec:data_leakage_lit}

Data leakage occurs when information from outside the operational training distribution is inadvertently incorporated into model optimization~\cite{kaufman2012leakage}. In machine-learning-based computational science, Kapoor and Narayanan~\cite{kapoor2023leakage} documented that data leakage represents the leading cause of systematic reproducibility failure, affecting hundreds of published papers across multiple academic disciplines.

In clinical machine learning, data leakage frequently manifests through non-independent record allocation across validation folds. Vandewiele et al.~\cite{vandewiele2021overly} highlighted that when tabular healthcare datasets duplicate identical instances or cluster closely related clinical encounters, random cross-validation yields grossly inflated, overly optimistic predictive scores. Similarly, Yagis et al.~\cite{yagis2021effect} demonstrated that record leakage across train and test partitions invalidates reported diagnostic boundaries in neuroimaging. 

In multi-disease classification from reported symptoms, public benchmark repositories—such as the widely benchmarked Kaggle Disease Prediction dataset~\cite{kaushil2020kaggle}—have repeatedly reported perfect 99\% to 100\% classification accuracies across published studies. However, prior literature has benchmarked these models naively without conducting independent forensic verification of duplicate instance rates or assessing the degree of train-test feature leakage.

\section{Feature Selection, Dimensionality Reduction, and Sparsity}
\label{sec:feature_selection_lit}

Clinical symptom checklists frequently encompass hundreds of potential signs, yet collecting exhaustive inventories imposes an untenable burden on patients and primary care clinicians. Feature selection is therefore essential to isolate high-yield clinical indicators while preserving predictive power~\cite{guyon2003introduction, saeys2007review}.

Feature selection methodologies fall into three primary algorithmic paradigms:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Filter Methods}: Information-theoretic techniques, such as the Minimal Redundancy Maximal Relevance (mRMR) framework introduced by Peng et al.~\cite{peng2005feature}, rank features based on Mutual Information with the target disease while penalizing pairwise correlation among selected symptoms.
    \item \textbf{Embedded Regularization}: The Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (Lasso) proposed by Tibshirani~\cite{tibshirani1996regression} applies an $L_1$ penalty to model coefficients, inducing continuous variable shrinkage that drives redundant symptom weights precisely to zero.
    \item \textbf{Ensemble Wrapper Methods}: Algorithmic frameworks such as Boruta~\cite{kursa2010feature} iteratively evaluate symptom importance against randomized shadow features generated via Random Forest permutations, capturing complex multi-way symptom interactions.
\end{enumerate}

Despite extensive algorithmic development, the literature exhibits a critical gap regarding how feature selection algorithms interact with leakage-aware validation partitions: feature selection applied to duplicated data frequently memorizes synthetic artifacts rather than biologically meaningful diagnostic discriminators.

\section{Probability Calibration and Uncertainty Quantification}
\label{sec:calibration_lit}

In high-stakes clinical decision support, raw classification accuracy is insufficient: automated systems must communicate trustworthy, well-calibrated posterior probabilities~\cite{shortliffe2018clinical}. A decision support tool that outputs a 90\% probability for Dengue or Typhoid must correspond to an empirical observation where approximately 90 out of 100 identically scored patients truly possess the condition.

Guo et al.~\cite{guo2017calibration} demonstrated that modern deep neural networks and complex boosted tree ensembles are routinely miscalibrated, exhibiting overconfident predictions even when misclassifying samples. To remediate this defect, post-hoc calibration methods have been formulated:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Platt Scaling}: Platt~\cite{platt1999probabilistic} introduced parametric logistic transformation to map uncalibrated model outputs into true posterior probabilities.
    \item \textbf{Isotonic Regression}: Zadrozny and Elkan~\cite{zadrozny2002transforming} proposed non-parametric isotonic regression, which fits a monotonically non-decreasing piecewise constant function, accommodating arbitrary score distortions without assuming parametric sigmoid geometry.
\end{itemize}

Empirical evaluations by Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana~\cite{niculescumizil2005predicting} demonstrated that Isotonic regression reliably corrects distortion in tree-based architectures and neural models with large sample sizes. Furthermore, calibration quality must be evaluated using strictly proper scoring rules~\cite{gneiting2007strictly}, notably the multi-class Brier score~\cite{brier1950verification} and Expected Calibration Error (ECE), ensuring that optimizing calibration does not degrade predictive discriminability.

\section{Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) and Physician Trust}
\label{sec:xai_lit}

The clinical integration of machine learning systems is fundamentally bounded by interpretability: clinicians cannot ethically act on black-box diagnostic recommendations without understanding the evidentiary basis~\cite{topol2019high, holzinger2019causability}. Rudin~\cite{rudin2019stop} argued forcefully that high-stakes domains must avoid uninterpretable black-box models in favor of inherently transparent architectures or rigorous explanation mechanisms.

Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME), formulated by Ribeiro et al.~\cite{ribeiro2016why}, builds linear surrogates within local perturbation neighborhoods. However, local surrogates can exhibit sampling instability across adjacent clinical runs. To establish mathematical consistency, Lundberg and Lee~\cite{lundberg2017unified} synthesized cooperative game theory and Shapley values into TreeSHAP, providing unique, additive feature attribution values satisfying local accuracy, missingness, and consistency axioms. Molnar~\cite{molnar2020interpretable} synthesized best practices for clinical XAI, noting that attribution explanations must not only be statistically stable across folds but must also align with formal diagnostic consensus and established pathophysiology.

\section{Differential Diagnosis and Multi-Candidate Clinical Triage}
\label{sec:differential_diagnosis_lit}

In real-world emergency triage and primary care practice, physicians rarely commit to an isolated single diagnosis during early patient intake; instead, they generate a prioritized \emph{differential diagnosis} encompassing top plausible etiologies~\cite{razzaki2018comparative}. Fraser et al.~\cite{fraser2018evaluating} and Ferraccioli et al.~\cite{ferraccioli2022differential} highlighted that evaluating automated diagnostic models strictly on Top-1 single-label accuracy produces a misleadingly pessimistic assessment of their assistive utility: if the true pathology is consistently ranked within the Top-3 or Top-5 differential candidates, the system safely guides secondary confirmatory investigations (e.g., blood cultures, serology, or imaging).

\section{Research Gaps Addressed by This Thesis}
\label{sec:research_gaps}

Synthesizing the surveyed literature, four unresolved research gaps emerge that this thesis directly investigates and resolves:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Gap 1: Absence of Forensic Leakage Auditing on Public Symptom Benchmarks}: Prior studies benchmark classifiers on public repositories (e.g., the Kaggle Disease Prediction dataset) without auditing duplicate rows, class boundary discrepancies, or train-test leakage. This thesis conducts the first code-verified forensic audit establishing exact duplication rates and leakage bounds.
    \item \textbf{Gap 2: Leakage-Aware Protocol Comparison}: The divergence between naive random cross-validation and strict duplicate-aware group partitioning (Protocol C) has not been evaluated across multi-family tabular classifiers.
    \item \textbf{Gap 3: Missing Robustness Profiling Under Progressive Clinical Sparsity and Noise}: Existing literature lacks controlled evaluations of how multi-class models degrade under realistic symptom omission budgets ($s \in [1, 10]$) and bidirectional reporting noise, as well as the compensatory power of Top-$k$ differential candidate ranking.
    \item \textbf{Gap 4: Empirical Medical Ground Truth Alignment}: Post-hoc XAI attributions are rarely cross-referenced against authoritative medical diagnostic literature. This thesis benchmarks SHAP attribution stability directly against clinical guidelines from the World Health Organization (WHO), CDC, ADA, and GINA.
\end{enumerate}
